Getting around on a phone

The bar along the bottom has five tabs. Map shows the full map. Species has "What's near me?", the A-Z species list, what's in season this month, and how species connect. Challenge has the Trail Challenge and the quiz. My Pins holds your own pins and photos. More has About & Visiting Info, Trails, By the Numbers, these instructions, and contact details. Each tab slides up as a sheet over the map: tap the same tab again, tap Map, tap the ✕, or drag the sheet down by its top edge to close it. On a computer, all of these sections are in the menu on the left instead.

A species card opens at half height so the map stays visible. Drag its top edge up to read it full screen, and down to shrink or close it; tapping the top edge switches between the two heights.

Getting around the map

Pan by dragging and zoom with the +/− buttons or a pinch gesture. Two icons sit at the top right of the map, and they control two different things. The layers icon (stacked squares) switches the base map between Satellite, OpenTopoMap, USGS Topo, and Street map, and toggles Trails and Areas on or off: it's for the map itself. The funnel icon next to it controls what's shown on top of that map: Type, Habitat, and Native status, plus Community Sightings and Community Picks. If you're looking to change what the map looks like, use layers; if you're looking to change which pins are showing, use the funnel. At the bottom right are the camera button (snap a photo pin), the location button (show where you are), and zoom. When you're zoomed out, nearby pins group into a numbered circle so the map doesn't get cluttered: zoom in, or tap the circle, to spread them back apart. Teal-ringed pins are Community Picks; plain pins are species verified in person for this guide.

Exploring species

Every species is in one A-Z list (under the Species tab on phones), grouped by Trees, Plants & Wildflowers, Animals, and so on. Tap a pin on the map or a name in the list to open its field notes, organized into tabs: Overview, Ecology, Uses (edible/medicinal, where relevant), Caution (only shown when there's a genuine hazard), and Connections (links to ecologically related species you can tap through to). Each card also shows a Habitat badge, a Native/Introduced/Invasive status badge, a Role chip summarizing its ecosystem job, and, for Community Picks, a teal badge and a credit box naming the observer.

The funnel button opens a panel with a "Show" section at the top (Community Sightings and Community Picks), followed by Type, Habitat, and Native status checkboxes. They all work together: a pin only shows when it passes every check. The panel also shows how many pins are showing, and Show all turns every filter back on.

Sharing

The Share button on any card sends a link that opens straight to that species. On a phone it opens your share sheet (text, email, social apps); on a computer it copies the link. The address bar also changes to match whichever card is open, so you can copy a link from there too. In the Trail Challenge, Share my progress makes an image of your found list to share or save.

Community Picks

Beyond the species verified in person for this guide, the "Community Picks" checkbox in the funnel panel adds real, iNaturalist-documented species into the same list, each marked with a teal ring on its map pin and a credit box on its card naming the observer and linking to the original observation. A pin here means the species was documented at that spot, not that it's guaranteed to still be there today. Uncheck the box to hide them and see only the field-verified species.

How It All Connects

This section (under the Species tab on phones) lists every real ecological relationship between two species in the guide: who eats whom, who pollinates or spreads whom, who depends on whom for food or shelter. Tap either name in a pair to jump straight to its card.

Trail Challenge

A self-guided checklist for your walk, under the Challenge tab on phones. "Starter 10" is a hand-picked set of species that are easy to recognize and represent the park's habitats and native/invasive story; "In season now" shows whatever matches the current month; "All" covers every species in the guide. Tap a box (here or the "I found this one" button on a card) to mark it found: your list is saved only on this device, and nothing is sent anywhere. Once you've found at least one on the list you're viewing, "Share my progress" makes an image of it. Reset clears your list.

Test Yourself

A short quiz (also under the Challenge tab on phones) generated fresh from the species cards each time, covering ecosystem roles, fun facts, scientific names, seasonal timing, and the links between species, so it never goes stale as content is added. Each answer comes with a short explanation and a link back to the card. If you've found at least 5 species in the Trail Challenge, you can choose to be quizzed only on those.

Ridgeway Recreation Area by the Numbers

A snapshot (under More on phones) of what naturalists have recorded here on iNaturalist: how many observations, how many species, which months see the most activity, and the most-recorded species overall. It's a real sample of what's been documented, not a full census, and it's refreshed periodically from a new export rather than updating live.

Trails & areas

Trails appear as dashed lines, listed under "Trails" (under More on phones) with their length: tap one to find it on the map. Shaded areas, where the guide has them, mark the boundary of a specific place (like a meadow or a habitat zone) rather than a walking path, listed under "Areas." Both can be turned on or off from the map's layer switcher.

Community sightings

This optional layer pulls in real, community-submitted observations from iNaturalist near the park - a separate thing from both the verified species and the Community Picks above, since these are raw individual sightings rather than field-guide writeups. Turn it on from the "Show" section in the funnel Filters panel, which then reveals a second set of checkboxes there to show just birds, plants, insects, and so on. Tap any dot to see the photo, date, and a link back to the original observation.

Finding your location

The location button (navigation arrow) near the zoom controls shows your position on the map as a blue dot and follows you as you walk. On phones with a compass, a blue arrow on the dot shows which way you're facing (iPhones ask permission for this the first time). "What's near me?" at the top of the species list shows the 15 closest pins with their distance and direction, updating as you walk, with a check mark on any you've already found: tap one to open its card. Location needs your permission the first time, only works on the live, secure website (not a locally saved file), and works best on a phone with GPS: accuracy will vary, especially under heavy tree cover. Your location is never sent anywhere.

If your location seems off

When you first turn location on, your phone often starts with a rough estimate from cell towers and Wi-Fi, sometimes hundreds of feet or more off, while GPS locks in. That can take anywhere from a few seconds to a couple of minutes, and it's slower under trees or right after you arrive somewhere new. While it's rough, a message at the bottom says "Finding GPS..." with how accurate the fix is so far, and the map waits for a good fix before moving; "GPS locked in" means it's there. Standing in the open for a moment helps, and so, sometimes, does briefly opening another map app on your phone.

Other map apps can be exact while this guide is rough because each app has its own location permission, and a web page gets location through your browser's. If the guide stays far off, make sure your browser is allowed precise location, then reload the page. Safari on iPhone: Settings, Privacy & Security, Location Services, Safari Websites, Precise Location. Chrome on iPhone: Settings, Chrome, Location, Precise Location. Android: Settings, Location, App location permissions, Chrome, Use precise location. If you opened the guide from a Home Screen icon, also try it in the browser itself, or the other way around, since they can have separate settings.

To see exactly what your phone is reporting, add #gps-check to the end of the guide's web address. It lists each reading with its accuracy, and a Copy report button lets you send the details to the guide's maintainer.

Saving your own pins

Long-press anywhere on the map (right-click on a computer) to drop a personal pin for something you've found. First, choose whether the pin should sit exactly where you pressed, or at your live GPS location instead (useful if your press was a rough guess but your actual position is more precise). Then pick Plant, Animal, or Not Sure, add a name or guess and any notes, and optionally a photo from your camera or library. Or tap the camera button on the map (or "Snap a photo pin" under My Pins) to take a picture first: the pin form opens with the photo attached and your GPS location selected. Your pins show up as solid red circles and are saved privately on this device. Pins with a photo show a thumbnail in the list and in the map popup: tap it to see the photo full screen.

From My Pins, you can:

Download for email (or Send, for pins with a photo): exports one pin (or all of them) to send to the park guide's maintainer for consideration. On phones, pins with photos open your share sheet so the note and photo go out together, for example in a new email; elsewhere the note and photo download as separate files. Photos are shrunk and stored only on this device.

Export/Upload My Data: a different pair of buttons for your own continuity. Export saves everything, photos included, to a file you keep, and Upload reads that file back in later, on this device or another one, adding anything new without creating duplicates.

Tip: on iPhone, add the guide to your Home Screen (Share, then Add to Home Screen). Safari can clear saved data for websites you haven't visited in a while, but not for guides added to the Home Screen, so this keeps your found list, pins, and photos safe.

This month at the park

The highlighted box shows whatever's currently flowering, fruiting, or otherwise in season, based on your device's own clock: tap an entry to jump straight to that species. On a computer it's near the top of the menu; on phones it's under the Species tab, below the list.

Using the guide offline

Cell signal can be weak around the lake, so this guide is built to work without a connection. Open it once while you have signal, then check Use Offline (under More on phones): it says "Ready" once the guide is saved on your device. After that, every species card, the Trail Challenge, the quiz, and your pins and photos work offline, and a banner tells you when you've lost signal.

Map tiles you've already looked at are kept. To be sure an area is there later, zoom to it while connected and tap Save this map view: it shows about how many tiles and megabytes that takes before saving. Remove saved tiles frees the space again, and Add to home screen keeps the guide like an app. The location dot and "What's near me?" use your phone's GPS, which usually keeps working without cell signal. Community Sightings show the last set that loaded, and sharing or sending pins waits until you have signal again.

Questions, comments, or suggestions

A "Questions, Comments & Suggestions" section (at the bottom of the menu, or under More on phones) has a direct email link for general feedback. If you'd like to suggest a specific plant or animal be added to the guide, save a personal pin and use its Download or Send option instead, so your coordinates, notes, and photo come through with it.

About & Visiting Info

The Ridgeway Recreation Area sits on Tails Creek at Carters Lake, a 3,200-acre U.S. Army Corps of Engineers reservoir in the Blue Ridge foothills of north Georgia. It has a boat ramp, a nature trail that crosses the creek on a wooden bridge, and a mountain bike loop. This guide is built from real visits and will grow over time.

About the Trails

The trail lines on the map come from OpenStreetMap (see Trails on the Map). The lengths and descriptions below are compiled from published sources and are approximate.

Tumbling Waters Nature Trail
About 1 mile or slightly more, out and back · Easy to moderate

Starts near the Ridgeway boat ramp and drops into shady forest with ferns and large hardwoods, then follows a small creek and climbs past a grassy meadow. After about 0.35 mile the trail splits. The left fork crosses a 150-foot bridge, 30 feet above Tails Creek, and continues upriver to an overlook right on the creek. The right fork also runs upriver to an overlook. The valley is described as a moist, steep-sided gorge with lush mountain vegetation, and two viewing platforms look out over Tails Creek cascading toward the lake. Hikers report the trail is marked with red diamonds.

Carters Lake Blue Trail
About 1.2 to 1.3 miles · Listed as easy

Listed on AllTrails within the recreation area. This trail is not confirmed by the Corps of Engineers, so check the signs at the trailhead for current details.

Mountain bike trails

Smooth Operator
2.1-mile loop · Intermediate

A flow trail with beginner and intermediate lines that merge into older singletrack in the middle. It starts at the top of the road down to the boat launch on the east side and can be ridden in either direction.

Roots & Chutes
0.4 mile · Very difficult

A short, technical trail rated very difficult by riders, with a gap jump at the end.

Ledges
0.4 mile · Difficult

A short trail rated difficult by riders.

Bike trail lengths and ratings come from MTB Project riders. Riders also report a newer connector called Big Dawg and a trail called Molla Rolla Loop, which are not on MTB Project's map.

Native vs. Invasive at This Park

Like much of the Southeast, the land around Carters Lake carries the fingerprints of centuries of human movement. Species introduced from Asia and Europe, often planted intentionally for ornamental beauty or erosion control, have spread well beyond where they were planted, and now compete with native species across Georgia. As species get added to this guide, look for the status badge on each card to see which is which.

Even a species with some redeeming value, like feeding pollinators or birds, can still cause harm when it spreads unchecked: a landscape dominated by a few aggressive newcomers supports less overall biodiversity than one built on a full community of native species.

Native Introduced Invasive

Look for this status badge on each species card, and a small warning dot on invasive species' map pins. Where relevant, we've noted a native alternative that fills a similar role. You can help track the spread of invasive species through Georgia's Early Detection & Distribution Mapping System (EDDMapS).
